Custom software vs. SaaS: when owning beats renting — and when it doesn't

Field Notes 02 · Decision framework · 9 min read · Corehold, Dubai

A studio that builds custom software telling you to buy custom software is not analysis — it's a sales pitch. So let's do this properly, starting with the cases against us. There are situations where SaaS is genuinely the right answer, and pretending otherwise is how businesses end up with expensive systems they didn't need. The skill isn't picking a side. It's knowing which situation you're in.

Where SaaS genuinely wins

S.1Commodity functions

Email hosting, accounting, payroll, video calls, document signing. These problems are identical across millions of companies, the vendors have poured decades into them, and nothing about how you do them differentiates your business. Building these yourself is vanity engineering. Rent them, forever, happily.

S.2Early-stage, still-changing processes

If your sales process changes every month, freezing it into custom software captures the wrong shape. SaaS is the right scaffolding while you discover how your business actually works. Build when the process has stabilized — not before.

S.3Temporary needs

A campaign tool for one quarter, software for a project that ends. Renting things you'll stop needing is exactly what renting is for.

S.4Genuinely small scale

Three people on a $50/month plan is not a problem to solve. The ownership math needs enough spend, enough seats, or enough friction to matter.

S.5Regulated, specialized verticals

Some vertical tools carry compliance machinery (medical records, aviation, banking cores) that took vendors years of certification. Sometimes the moat is real. Respect it.

Where ownership wins — usually by more than anyone expects

O.1The process is the business

How you quote, sell, deliver, and decide — the operational spine that makes you different from competitors. Running your differentiation on the same rented tool your competitor uses erases it. Software shaped around your actual process doesn't just fit better; it compounds your advantage every year it runs.

O.2The integration tax has arrived

The clearest signal: you're paying for middleware, automation tools, and human copy-pasting just to make your other tools talk to each other. That's not a stack anymore — it's a tax. When glue costs rival tool costs, one connected system beats ten disconnected ones on price alone, before counting the errors that disappear.

O.3Per-seat pricing is scaling against you

SaaS pricing punishes growth: double your team, double the rent. Owned systems invert this — the build cost is fixed, so every new seat makes ownership cheaper per person. Somewhere between twenty and a hundred people, most stacks quietly cross this line without anyone noticing.

O.4Your data has gravity

When decisions depend on seeing sales, operations, and finance together — and today that picture lives in six vendor silos with export limits — ownership isn't a preference, it's the prerequisite. This matters double in the AI era: you cannot build intelligence on data you don't control. (More on that in FN.03.)

O.5Vendor risk has become business risk

A price hike you can't refuse, a feature sunset that breaks your workflow, an acquisition that kills the product. If a stranger's roadmap decision could damage your operation, your foundation doesn't belong to you.

Rent what makes you the same as everyone. Own what makes you different.

The five-year lens

The comparison most businesses run — this month's subscription vs. a custom build quote — is the wrong one. The honest frame is five years of total cost against what exists at the end. Five years of a $3,700/month stack is roughly $221,000, rising with headcount and price ratchets, terminating in nothing owned (we did that arithmetic in FN.01). A custom system carries its cost up front, then declines to hosting and maintenance — and terminates in an asset: code, data, and infrastructure that keep working without permission. SaaS spend is flat rent forever. Ownership is a curve that bends in your favor around year two and never bends back.

The Corehold position We don't begin engagements with a build recommendation. We begin with an audit of how the business actually operates — and sometimes the finding is "your stack is fine; stop at two changes." A studio you can trust with your foundation has to be able to say no. When the finding is that ownership pays, we show the math first, in your numbers, using the same five-year lens above.

How to decide this week, not this quarter

List your subscriptions and mark each one: commodity or core? For the core items, ask three questions. Does this tool shape how we compete? Are we paying glue costs to connect it to everything else? Would losing it tomorrow damage the business? Three yeses on any core system is the ownership threshold — the point where renting has become the expensive option, whatever the monthly fee says.
